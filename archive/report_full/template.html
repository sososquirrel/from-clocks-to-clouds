Clock Koopman VAE

A VAE learns to compress images of clocks. A Koopman loss then forces its latent space to evolve linearly in time. This report compares the two latent spaces, the spectrum of the learned operator K, and how far into the future each model can predict.

Setup

Data, model and loss

rendered clock images (). Hour and minute hands start at independent random angles, so the true state is two angles on a torus. One step is one minute: the minute hand turns 6° and the hour hand 0.5°.

Stage 1. A convolutional VAE with a -dimensional latent, trained on single images with reconstruction + KL. It has no notion of time.

Stage 2. Starting from the stage-1 weights, a learnable matrix K (initialised to the identity) is added. On runs x₀ … x_H of consecutive minutes, with μₖ the encoder mean:

Stage 1 up close

How the VAE sees a clock

The encoder reads a 64×64 image and outputs two vectors of 16 numbers: a mean μ and a spread σ. During training the decoder receives a noisy sample z = μ + σ·ε with ε ~ N(0, 1), and must redraw the clock from it. Two terms pull against each other:

loss = reconstruction  +  β · KL( q(z|x) ‖ N(0, I) )          β = 1
       ≈  nats            ≈  nats   per image, end of training
reconstruction: how many nats of the image the decoder still gets wrong (binary cross-entropy over 4,096 pixels)
KL:             how far each clock's blob q(z|x) = N(μ, σ²) sits from the shared prior N(0, I)

Generating new clocks

A VAE is also a generative model. The decoder never needs an input image: give it any 16 numbers z and it draws a clock. Each generated clock below is labelled with the real time it is closest to, found by searching every hand position. A dashed frame marks samples that are not a clean clock (pixel error to the closest real clock above 1×10⁻³).

Why the plain prior struggles here: the clocks form a torus (two independent circles) inside the 16-D latent. The KL term pulls each clock's blob towards N(0, I) on average, but a torus has a hole, and the centre of the prior falls into it. Latents drawn there have no clock to decode to, so the decoder blends several, which is why shrinking the prior (0.5²) makes samples worse, not better. Drawing z from the encoder's own blobs, μ + σ·ε of real clocks, stays on the torus and gives clean new clocks. So does a latent whose shape matches the data: in the minimal Koopman model the latent is two circles, and one random angle per circle is always a clock.

Minimal Koopman

Four numbers, two rotations

The 16-D model uses 7 rotating modes, mostly harmonics. Four dimensions are enough in principle: one complex number per hand, and K made of two 2×2 rotation blocks. Trained on its own, a 4-D model got stuck on harmonics (runs 1–4 in the log). This one was obtained in two steps:

1. Distil. A fresh 4-D encoder and decoder are trained to reproduce the 16-D model's two hand eigenfunctions, φ0.5° for the hour hand and φ18°·φ̄12° for the minute hand. K is set by least squares on those coordinates and kept fixed.

2. Fine-tune. The teacher is dropped. Encoder, decoder and K train on the plain Koopman VAE loss, with runs up to 36 minutes long.

Theory

Harmonics, explained

Why does the model learn 12° and 18° for a hand that turns 6° per minute, and how many latent dimensions does a clock really need? Everything follows from one fact: every harmonic of a hand is a Koopman eigenfunction.

1. What a harmonic is

Harmonic k of a hand is the complex number φₖ = e^{ikθ}: a point on a circle at k times the hand's angle. When the hand makes one turn, φₖ makes k turns. k = 1 (the fundamental) tells you exactly where the hand is. k = 2 cannot tell θ from θ + 180°, k = 3 cannot tell θ from θ + 120°, and so on.

2. Why every harmonic is a Koopman mode

one step:   θ → θ + ω                      (ω = 0.5° hour, 6° minute)
harmonic:   φₖ(θ + ω) = e^{ik(θ+ω)} = e^{ikω} · φₖ(θ)
⇒ φₖ is an eigenfunction of the Koopman operator, eigenvalue λ = e^{ikω}: a rotation by k·ω per step.
both hands: φ_pq = e^{i(p θ_h + q θ_m)},  λ = e^{i(p ω_h + q ω_m)} = e^{i (p + 12q) · 0.5°}

So the Koopman spectrum of a clock contains infinitely many valid modes. The loss only asks for linear evolution and good images, and any harmonic satisfies the first. Which ones end up in the latent depends on how much of the image they explain and how easy they are to reach.

3. How much of the image each harmonic carries

Fourier analysis of the images over all hand positions (3° grid): the share of all image variation carried by each harmonic. Only involves both hands at once; the rest belongs to one hand or the other.

hour handminute hand
What each harmonic looks like as an image: the pattern added to the clock when φₖ = 1 (red = more ink, blue = less).

The hour hand's k = 2 is the single strongest term of all, ahead of its fundamental: each hand has a short tail on the opposite side, so the image is almost unchanged by a half turn. The thin minute hand spreads its signal over many harmonics; its fundamental is only the 10th strongest term overall.

4. Linear readout: you need many harmonics

If the decoder were linear (classic DMD / EDMD), the image would have to be a weighted sum of the stored eigenfunctions. Here is one clock rebuilt from the n strongest harmonic pairs, using d = 2n + 1 latent numbers.

5. Deep decoder: you need a basis

A nonlinear decoder does not need the image as a sum; it only needs to know which clock it is. A set of harmonics identifies every clock exactly when their frequency vectors (p, q) can build every integer pair by integer combinations, i.e. they generate ℤ². Two modes (4 numbers) are enough; one mode never is. Click lattice points to choose a set, or pick a preset.

The diagonal lines join modes that share an eigenvalue: λ depends only on p + 12q because the minute hand turns exactly 12 times faster. On the bold line, p + 12q = 0, the eigenvalue is 1: c = θ_m − 12 θ_h never changes, a conserved quantity. That is why "hour hand + c" is also a valid minimal 4-D solution.

To prefer the fundamentals, the loss needs a tie-breaker, for example a slowness penalty (slow feature analysis): harmonic k moves k times faster, so the slowest coordinates that still identify the clock are the fundamentals. Because of the 12 : 1 ratio, the slowest of all is the conserved c, so slowness alone should give "hour hand + c". That experiment is running.

Experiment

Choosing the modes: a slowness prior

The Koopman loss accepts any set of harmonics that identifies the clock, so on its own it has no reason to pick the fundamentals. A tie-breaker from slow feature analysis: penalise how far the latent code moves in one minute,

L_slow = mean ‖μ_{t+1} − μ_t‖² / Var(μ)          added to the loss with weight w_slow

Harmonic k of a hand turns k times faster than the fundamental, so it costs about k² times more slowness. Among the coordinates that still identify the clock, the cheapest are the fundamentals (and, because of the 12 : 1 ratio, the conserved c).

One row per model: eigenvalues of K, the best hour and minute eigenfunction (held-out clocks coloured by the true angle), a 6-hour rollout from one image, and pixel error against the horizon.

What it shows. Strong slowness does select the minute fundamental (6.007° per step, one clean circle) and removes the fast harmonics. It pays for it in accuracy: reconstructions are about 13× worse and the hour speed is off by about 25%, so long rollouts drift. Weaker slowness keeps images closer to sharp, but the harmonics return once the Koopman loss takes over. In d = 4 no variant escaped the trap.

Take-away. Interpretable modes and sharp predictions pull in different directions under this loss. The distilled 4-D model shows that both can coexist; gradient descent just does not find that solution unaided.

Mixed modes, natural hands

The two learned modes are not "hour" and "minute". They are α = θ_h (mode (1, 0), 0.504° per minute) and β = θ_h + θ_m (mode (1, 1), 6.503° per minute). Both rotate at constant speed, and together they identify every clock, so reconstruction and prediction are just as good as with the natural hands. The hands are one change of basis away: θ_h = α and θ_m = β − α, which turns at 6.503° − 0.504° = 5.999° per minute.

Turning the coordinates in latent space and decoding shows what each one means:

Why the network chose (1, 1) rather than (0, 1) is not explained by the loss: both are equally good for it, and the result held over every seed, slowness weight and initialisation we tried. Koopman fixes the spectrum (here 0.5° and 6°, read from the eigenvalues), not which basis of it the latent stores. Getting one mode per hand directly needs an extra assumption, for example a decoder that draws each mode's part of the image separately.

Fast experiments

Small latents, trained from scratch

A faster setup to iterate: 32×32 images, half-width networks, 16 clips per batch, horizon up to 36 minutes. A Koopman run takes about a minute instead of half an hour. The latent-loss weight is scaled down (125 instead of 500) because the images have 4× fewer pixels. Images are blurrier at this size, so compare these models with each other rather than with the full 64-px model.

The earlier full-size d = 4 runs failed because they had 5-minute steps, a short horizon and an over-weighted latent loss.

Spectrum

Eigenvalues of the learned operator

One step is one minute, so a mode turning θ per step closes one loop in 360°/θ steps. A pure rotation of θ per step has eigenvalues e±iθ on the unit circle. A harmonic eikθ of a hand is also a Koopman eigenfunction, with eigenvalue e±ikθ. Points inside the circle decay, which shows up as blur over time.

Three lessons

The step size decides what can be found. Gradient descent moves K only a little from the identity. A 30° rotation per step is out of reach, while 6° is not. A 5-minute tick is then simply K⁵.

Harmonics compete for latent room. Thin hands give each pixel a spiky dependence on the angle, so the harmonics of a hand explain as much of the image as its fundamental. With a small latent, harmonics of one hand crowd out the other hand.

A one-step loss cannot pin down slow modes. Over a single minute, 0.1° and 0.5° look the same. A multi-step loss compares Kᵏz₀ with frames up to 24 minutes ahead, so small eigenvalue errors add up until the loss sees them.
